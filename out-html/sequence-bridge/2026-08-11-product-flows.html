The product at three altitudes

What the patient experiences, what we install, and what we do every week to keep it running — with the gaps marked rather than papered over.

step — patient or practice step — the sequence acts dead end, no v1 coverage milestone

Layer 1 — moves with the offer

The patient journey

What a patient actually experiences. This is the layer that maps most directly onto landing page copy and the sales conversation, because it is the thing we sell.

Where the value sits. Everything between lead captured and shows up is the product. We don't create the demand at the top, and we don't touch the clinical work inside the visit.

Calls are in v1 because of the denominator, not the feature. Speed-to-lead, booking rate, and cost per booked appointment all divide by "inquiries." A form-only version computes every headline metric on a partial denominator — and it flatters us, because form leads that got an instant reply convert well. Handing an owner a booking rate that ignores the calls they know they missed is how the first monthly report loses credibility, and the first report is what the month-3 renewal turns on. Call data also has no backfill.

The residual gap is deliberate. Rerouting a practice's primary revenue channel two weeks into a relationship is the riskiest thing we could ask for. Our number is additive; if it ever breaks, their old one still rings.

Layer 2 — moves with the code

What we install

The system a signed practice actually gets, and where our boundary sits. One multi-tenant app we own and operate, so an added practice costs close to nothing at the margin.

No PHI crosses the boundary. We hold contact details and booking preferences. Everything clinical stays in the practice's PMS, which keeps the first cohort out of HIPAA scope and constrains every spec until deliberately revisited.

A2P 10DLC gates the SMS rail, not the voice one. The tracking number is a Twilio voice number and needs no registration, so adding call capture doesn't extend the launch critical path. Missed-call text-back then reuses the account, the templates, and the scheduler — the marginal build is a forwarding rule plus a status callback, with STOP language in the template.

The dashboard is not a nice-to-have. It's the artifact the retainer is sold against. If per-client metrics aren't native to the platform, the monthly report becomes manual work that scales linearly with clients and eats the margin the multi-tenant bet is supposed to produce.

Layer 3 — moves with operations

The client lifecycle

What happens on our side, from first conversation to the renewal decision. This is the layer that justifies the retainer, and the one that's easiest to underestimate.

Onboarding runs in five parallel tracks, and A2P is the long pole. The other four are work we control and can compress; registration is a queue we wait in. The 14-day launch commitment fails on that step or not at all. Provisioning the tracking number takes minutes and needs no 10DLC, so it adds a track without moving the deadline.

Discovery captures the call-vs-form baseline on every deal. One question — how many new-patient calls and form submissions did you get last month — and most practices can pull call volume from their phone provider on the spot. It's a step in the process rather than a task someone has to remember, and it's the data that settles open question 2.

The renewal decision is the whole experiment. Percentage of clients still paying past month 3 is the key success metric, because practices don't keep paying a four-figure monthly retainer for something that isn't producing. Target for the first cohort is 2 of 3.

Unresolved

Five things we haven't decided

Each one changes a diagram above. Listed here so scope changes aren't made blind.

1 · What do we do about the PMS?

What it is. The practice management system is the operational core of a dental practice — Dentrix and Eaglesoft dominate the independent market, with Open Dental, Curve, Denticon and CareStack behind them. It holds patient records, clinical charting, insurance claims, billing, and the part that matters to us: the appointment book. Dental scheduling isn't a simple calendar — appointments are assigned to a specific operatory and provider, with lengths that vary by procedure.

Practically every practice has one. Insurance claims and clinical record-keeping require it. This is not a system some practices skip.

The problem is bigger than we first wrote it. We framed this as how a booking gets into the PMS. The harder half is reading out of it. Layer 2 draws the practice calendar as a system separate from the PMS — in dental that's usually false. The calendar is the PMS. Without a read path we don't know real availability, so "booking against the practice's real availability" is a claim we currently can't support.

The risk isn't extra work, it's double-booking. If we book a patient into a shadow calendar while the front desk fills that slot in the PMS, a patient arrives to no chair. That's materially worse than admin work — it damages the practice in front of its own patient.

  • Request-to-book. Instant reply with proposed times; the front desk confirms in the PMS. Zero integration and honest, but a human sits in the path — the thing we sell against.
  • Hold slots. The practice reserves a few new-patient slots a day, blocked in the PMS, that only we book into. Instant booking is real, double-booking is structurally impossible, and transcription is bounded to a daily digest. Best v1 answer.
  • Middleware. One API across several PMSs — Sikka, or NexHealth's Synchronizer. Real availability and writeback, at a per-practice cost and a vendor dependency. NexHealth also competes with us for this budget.
  • Direct integration, Open Dental first. The most integration-friendly common system. Cheapest real path, but it constrains who we can sell to.

Should we target practices without a PMS? No — that set is effectively empty, and a practice without one isn't a practice we want as a pilot. The useful version of the instinct is to qualify on which PMS. Add PMS brand to discovery alongside the call-volume baseline; if the first cohort clusters on one system, that picks the v1.1 integration target for free.

Recommendation. Hold slots for v1, no integration, the transcription step stated plainly during the sale. Vendor specifics above come from general knowledge rather than a current survey — verify integration terms and middleware pricing before committing.

2 · How much of the phone channel does v1 need?

v1 covers a tracking number plus missed-call text-back; call answering and recording stay out. The decision rests on an unmeasured assumption — that phone is the majority inquiry channel for single-location dental. Reverses if calls turn out to be a small minority across the first cohort, since the argument for including them is volume and denominator integrity, and both collapse without the volume.

3 · What happens after a no-show?

Reminders reduce no-shows; they don't eliminate them. We report show rate, so we hand clients a number with no lever attached to it.

4 · Where does a referral land, and is it attributed?

The referral ask is an in-scope pillar, but the return path isn't tagged. A referred patient entering through the same untagged form makes the pillar unprovable — and unprovable value is the first thing cut at renewal.

5 · Where does cost-per-booked-appointment get its cost input?

We don't run ads or own lead volume, so the spend figure has to come from the practice. Confirm they'll share it before promising the metric.
